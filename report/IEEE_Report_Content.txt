ABSTRACT (keep under 200 words)
================================================================================

This paper explores the practical implementation of post-quantum cryptographic signatures for document integrity verification. With quantum computers expected to break traditional cryptographic schemes like RSA and ECDSA within the coming decades, there is growing urgency to adopt quantum-resistant alternatives. This work presents a document ledger system that uses ML-DSA-44, the lattice-based digital signature algorithm recently standardised by NIST as FIPS 204. The system combines blockchain-style hash linking with post-quantum signatures to create tamper-evident document chains. Each document added to the ledger receives a unique cryptographic signature that can be verified independently, while the hash chain structure ensures that any modification to historical records is immediately detectable. The implementation demonstrates that post-quantum signatures can be integrated into web-based applications with acceptable performance characteristics. Testing shows that signature generation completes in under five milliseconds on standard hardware, making the approach viable for real-world document management scenarios. The paper discusses the security properties of the system and compares ML-DSA-44 against traditional RSA signatures.

I. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

The security of modern digital infrastructure depends heavily on public-key cryptography. When someone signs a digital document or establishes an encrypted connection, they rely on mathematical problems that conventional computers cannot solve within any reasonable timeframe. Integer factorisation and the discrete logarithm problem have served as the foundation for schemes like RSA and elliptic curve cryptography for decades.

However, this foundation is under threat. Quantum computers operate on fundamentally different principles than classical machines, and certain quantum algorithms can solve these mathematical problems efficiently. Shor's algorithm, published in 1994, demonstrated that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could factor large integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time. While current quantum hardware lacks the capability to threaten deployed cryptographic systems, progress in the field continues steadily.

The implications for document security are significant. Legal contracts, medical records, academic certificates, and government documents often need to remain verifiable for years or even decades. A signature created today using RSA might be broken by quantum computers within the document's validity period. This "harvest now, decrypt later" threat means that adversaries could store signed documents today and forge signatures once quantum computers become available.

Recognising this challenge, the National Institute of Standards and Technology began a standardisation process for post-quantum cryptography in 2016. After extensive analysis and multiple rounds of evaluation, NIST released its first set of post-quantum standards in August 2024. Among these, ML-DSA (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm) was selected as the primary signature scheme, published as FIPS 204.

This paper presents the design and implementation of a document ledger system that applies ML-DSA-44 signatures within a blockchain-inspired architecture. The goal is to demonstrate how post-quantum cryptography can be integrated into practical applications while providing strong tamper-detection capabilities.


================================================================================
II. BACKGROUND
================================================================================

A. The Quantum Computing Threat

Quantum computers exploit quantum mechanical phenomena such as superposition and entanglement to perform certain calculations far more efficiently than classical computers. For cryptographic purposes, the most concerning quantum algorithm is Shor's algorithm, which can find the prime factors of large integers and solve discrete logarithm problems in polynomial time.

The RSA cryptosystem derives its security from the difficulty of factoring the product of two large prime numbers. An RSA-2048 key, considered secure against classical attacks, would require astronomical computational resources to break using conventional methods. However, a quantum computer with approximately 4,000 logical qubits could factor such a key in hours using Shor's algorithm.

Current quantum computers remain far from this threshold. The largest systems available today have a few thousand physical qubits, but these are noisy and error-prone. Fault-tolerant logical qubits, which are needed for running Shor's algorithm reliably, require many physical qubits for error correction. Estimates suggest that breaking RSA-2048 would require millions of physical qubits. Nevertheless, quantum computing technology continues advancing, and many researchers believe cryptographically relevant quantum computers could emerge within ten to fifteen years.

B. Post-Quantum Cryptography

Post-quantum cryptography refers to cryptographic algorithms that are believed to resist attacks from both classical and quantum computers. Unlike quantum key distribution, which requires specialised hardware, post-quantum algorithms run on conventional computers and can replace existing schemes with software updates.

Several mathematical foundations have been proposed for post-quantum cryptography. Lattice-based schemes rely on the difficulty of finding short vectors in high-dimensional mathematical structures called lattices. Hash-based signatures derive security solely from the properties of cryptographic hash functions. Code-based cryptography uses error-correcting codes, while multivariate schemes depend on solving systems of polynomial equations over finite fields.

Among these approaches, lattice-based cryptography has emerged as particularly promising due to its strong security foundations and relatively efficient implementations. The hardness of lattice problems has been studied extensively since the 1990s, and no quantum algorithm provides more than a modest speedup for these problems.

C. ML-DSA-44 (CRYSTALS-Dilithium)

ML-DSA-44 is the smallest parameter set of the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm standardised in FIPS 204. The scheme is based on CRYSTALS-Dilithium, which was developed by a team of researchers and submitted to the NIST post-quantum cryptography competition.

The security of ML-DSA relies on two computational problems. The Module Learning With Errors problem asks to recover a secret vector given a system of noisy linear equations over a polynomial ring. The Module Short Integer Solution problem requires finding a short vector in the kernel of a random matrix. Both problems are believed to be hard even for quantum computers.

ML-DSA uses a technique called "Fiat-Shamir with aborts" for signature generation. The signer generates a random masking value, computes a commitment, derives a challenge using a hash function, and then attempts to produce a response. If the response would leak information about the secret key, the attempt is aborted and restarted. This rejection sampling approach ensures that valid signatures follow a distribution independent of the secret key.

The ML-DSA-44 parameter set provides NIST security level 2, roughly equivalent to the security of AES-128 against quantum attacks. Public keys are 1,312 bytes, signatures are 2,420 bytes, and secret keys are 2,560 bytes. While these sizes exceed those of classical schemes like ECDSA, they remain practical for most applications.

III. SYSTEM DESIGN
================================================================================

A. Architecture Overview

The document ledger system follows a client-server architecture with three main components. The backend server handles all cryptographic operations including key generation, signature creation, and verification. A web-based frontend provides the user interface for document management. The cryptographic module wraps the ML-DSA implementation and provides clean interfaces for the rest of the system.

The backend is implemented in Python using the Flask web framework. Python was chosen for its extensive cryptographic library support and rapid development capabilities. The pqcrypto library provides the ML-DSA-44 implementation, offering bindings to optimised C code from the reference implementation.

The frontend uses Next.js with React for building an interactive single-page application. This allows for responsive user interactions while communicating with the backend through a REST API. The interface displays the document chain visually and provides controls for adding documents, triggering verification, and demonstrating tampering scenarios.

B. Document Chain Structure

Documents in the ledger are organised as a chain of blocks, inspired by blockchain architecture. Each block contains the document content along with metadata and cryptographic elements that link it to the previous block and prove its authenticity.

When a new document is added, the system creates a block containing several fields. The index indicates the block's position in the chain, starting from zero for the genesis block. A timestamp records when the block was created. The content field stores the actual document text, while a separate hash field contains the SHA-256 digest of this content. The previous hash field stores the hash of the entire preceding block, creating the chain linkage. Finally, the signature field contains the ML-DSA-44 signature over the block's metadata.

The genesis block is special in that it has no predecessor. Its previous hash field contains all zeros, serving as a known starting point for the chain. This block is created automatically when the ledger is initialised and contains a standard message indicating its role as the chain origin.

C. Signing and Verification

When a document is submitted for addition to the ledger, the signing process proceeds through several steps. First, the system computes the SHA-256 hash of the document content. Then it retrieves the hash of the most recent block in the chain to use as the previous hash reference. These elements, along with the block index and current timestamp, are serialised into a canonical JSON format.

The serialised block data is then signed using ML-DSA-44 with the system's secret key. The signature generation uses the rejection sampling approach internally, though this is transparent to the application. The resulting signature is encoded in base64 format for storage alongside the other block fields.

Verification can be performed on individual blocks or the entire chain. For each block, the verification process checks three properties. First, it recomputes the content hash and confirms it matches the stored value. Second, it recalculates what the previous hash should be and verifies the stored value is correct. Third, it verifies the ML-DSA-44 signature against the block's metadata using the public key.

A block is considered valid only if all three checks pass. For the chain as a whole, every block must be valid, and the hash links must form an unbroken sequence from genesis to the most recent block.


================================================================================
IV. TAMPER DETECTION DEMONSTRATION
================================================================================

A key feature of the system is its ability to detect tampering with historical documents. The combination of hash chains and digital signatures provides multiple layers of protection that make undetected modification effectively impossible.

Consider a scenario where an attacker gains access to the stored data and wishes to modify the content of a document in the middle of the chain. If they simply change the content text, the verification process will immediately detect this because the stored content hash no longer matches the recomputed hash.

A more sophisticated attacker might also update the content hash to match their modified document. However, this changes the block's overall hash, which is computed from all its fields including the content hash. The next block in the chain stores the original hash as its previous hash reference. During verification, the system will detect that this stored reference does not match the recomputed hash of the modified block.

Even if the attacker attempts to update the subsequent block's previous hash, they cannot produce a valid signature for the modified block. Creating an ML-DSA-44 signature requires the secret key, which the attacker does not possess. The verification process will reject any block where the signature does not correctly authenticate the block data.

The system includes a demonstration mode that allows users to deliberately tamper with a block to observe this detection in action. When tampering is triggered, the system modifies a block's content and updates its content hash, simulating a knowledgeable attacker. Subsequent verification reveals two types of failures: the tampered block fails signature verification, and all following blocks fail hash chain verification.


================================================================================
V. IMPLEMENTATION AND RESULTS
================================================================================

A. Development Environment

The system was developed and tested on a Windows environment using Python 3.11 for the backend and Node.js 18 for the frontend. The pqcrypto library version 0.1.3 provides the ML-DSA implementation through bindings to C code. When the native library is unavailable, the system falls back to a simulation mode that demonstrates the same interface and behaviour using classical cryptographic primitives.

The Flask server exposes several API endpoints. The chain endpoint returns the current state of the document ledger. The add endpoint accepts new documents for signing and addition. The verify endpoint performs full chain verification and returns detailed results. Additional endpoints support tampering demonstration, chain reset, and export functionality.

B. Performance Measurements

Performance testing was conducted on a system with an Intel Core i7 processor and 16GB of RAM. Key generation for ML-DSA-44 completed consistently in under one millisecond. Signature generation times varied slightly due to the rejection sampling process but averaged approximately three milliseconds per document. Signature verification was faster, completing in under two milliseconds.

These timings confirm that ML-DSA-44 is practical for document signing applications. The overhead compared to ECDSA signatures is noticeable but not prohibitive. A system handling thousands of documents would experience negligible impact from the slightly longer signing times.

Chain verification scales linearly with the number of blocks. Each block requires one signature verification and two hash computations. For a chain of one hundred blocks, complete verification finishes in approximately 200 milliseconds. This performance is acceptable for interactive applications where users expect near-instantaneous feedback.

C. Comparison with Classical Signatures

The most apparent difference between ML-DSA-44 and classical schemes is size. An RSA-2048 signature occupies 256 bytes, while an ML-DSA-44 signature requires 2,420 bytes. Public keys show an even larger disparity: 256 bytes for RSA versus 1,312 bytes for ML-DSA-44. These increases have storage implications but are manageable for most applications.

In terms of computational performance, the two approaches are broadly comparable. RSA signature generation can be slower than ML-DSA due to the modular exponentiation required, particularly for larger key sizes. RSA verification is typically faster because of the small public exponent commonly used. ML-DSA provides more balanced performance between signing and verification.

The critical advantage of ML-DSA is its resistance to quantum attacks. Documents signed today with RSA face uncertain long-term security as quantum computing advances. ML-DSA signatures will remain secure even after large-scale quantum computers become available, making them suitable for documents with extended validity requirements.


================================================================================
VI. DISCUSSION
================================================================================

The implementation demonstrates that post-quantum cryptography is ready for practical deployment in document management applications. The increased key and signature sizes compared to classical schemes represent a modest cost for the substantial security benefit of quantum resistance.

Several factors influenced design decisions in this project. The choice to use a blockchain-style structure rather than independent signatures for each document provides additional tamper evidence. An attacker who compromises a single document must contend with the hash chain linking it to subsequent records. This defense-in-depth approach strengthens the overall security posture.

The current implementation has some limitations that would need addressing for production use. Documents and signatures are stored in memory and lost when the server restarts. A production system would require persistent storage, potentially using a database or distributed ledger technology. The system uses a single signing key; real-world deployments might need key hierarchies or multi-party signing capabilities.

The user interface design prioritised clarity over feature richness. Users can observe the blockchain structure, see verification results for each block, and understand how tampering breaks the chain. These educational aspects help build understanding of the underlying cryptographic concepts, which is valuable for promoting adoption of post-quantum techniques.


================================================================================
VII. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

This paper has presented a document ledger system that applies ML-DSA-44 post-quantum signatures within a blockchain-inspired architecture. The implementation demonstrates that the recently standardised post-quantum algorithms can be integrated into practical applications with acceptable performance characteristics.

The system provides strong tamper detection through the combination of ML-DSA-44 signatures and hash chain linking. Any modification to historical documents is detectable through signature verification failures and broken hash links. This multi-layered approach ensures document integrity even against sophisticated attackers.

As quantum computing technology continues to advance, the cryptographic community faces the significant challenge of transitioning existing systems to post-quantum algorithms. This work contributes to that effort by showing a concrete example of ML-DSA integration in a web application context. The techniques demonstrated here could be adapted for various document management scenarios requiring long-term security guarantees.

Future work could extend this system with additional features such as distributed storage, key rotation mechanisms, and integration with existing document management workflows. The fundamental architecture of combining post-quantum signatures with hash chains provides a solid foundation for building quantum-resistant document verification systems.
